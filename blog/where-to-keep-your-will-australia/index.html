Where Should You Keep Your Will in Australia?

Published 25 September 2026 by Will Kimak.

Keep the signed original of your will somewhere safe, and make sure the person you have named as executor knows exactly where it is. Moneysmart, ASIC's consumer site, says to store your will in a safe place and tell someone you trust where to find it, and it names a fireproof safe at home, your solicitor, the courts or the Public Trustee as the usual places. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026) The part most people underestimate is that the paper original does real work here, and that the options open to you depend on which state or territory you live in. Both are covered below.

Does the original will matter, or is a photocopy enough?

The original matters. When an executor applies for probate, the court generally wants the will itself, and the court then keeps it. The Supreme Court of Victoria answers the question in its probate FAQs in four words: yes, the Court keeps the original will. (Source: supremecourt.vic.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)

The Supreme Court of New South Wales describes the same practice from the other end. Following a grant, it says, the Court retains the original will and all documents filed in support of the application. (Source: supremecourt.nsw.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026) The Supreme Court of Western Australia is blunter still, telling people that it will not return the original will, and that it retains the original as a public document in safe custody. (Source: supremecourt.wa.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)

So the document sitting in your filing cabinet is not one copy among several. It is the thing your executor has to produce.

What happens if the original cannot be found?

This is the reason storage is worth ten minutes of your attention. Where a will was last known to be in the will maker's own hands and cannot be found after their death, the court does not simply accept a photocopy. It starts from the assumption that the will maker destroyed it on purpose.

The Supreme Court of Victoria states the rule plainly: generally, where a person held their will but it cannot be found on their death, the Court will presume the testator destroyed it with intention to revoke it. (Source: supremecourt.vic.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026) The Victoria Law Handbook, published by Fitzroy Legal Service, puts the same doctrine in everyday words, saying that if a will disappears in the custody of the will maker, there is a rebuttable presumption that the will has been revoked by destruction. (Source: thelawhandbook.org.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)

Rebuttable is the important word. The presumption can be argued away, but somebody has to do the arguing, and that somebody is your executor. In Victoria, applying to prove a copy of a will means filing an affidavit of due execution from the witnesses where possible, evidence of the will's last known whereabouts, an account of the searches that were made, and evidence relied on to rebut the presumption of revocation. (Source: supremecourt.vic.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)

That is a slower and more expensive application than a straightforward one. A lost original does not automatically mean the will fails, and it does mean your family may have to prove something that a findable piece of paper would have settled on its own.

Can you deposit your will with the Supreme Court?

In some places, yes, usually for a fee, and this genuinely varies across the country.

In New South Wales, the Supreme Court says any person can voluntarily deposit their will with it for safe keeping on payment of the relevant fee. (Source: supremecourt.nsw.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026) Victoria has a similar arrangement, with the Probate Office accepting wills for deposit for a fee under section 5A of the Administration and Probate Act. (Source: thelawhandbook.org.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)

The Australian Capital Territory has gone the other way. Its Supreme Court says that as of 11 June 2025 it no longer provides a service for the depositing and storing of wills, and that wills previously deposited with the Court are being progressively transferred to the Public Trustee and Guardian. (Source: courts.act.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)

We have not been able to confirm a current court deposit service in Queensland, South Australia, Western Australia, Tasmania or the Northern Territory from those registries' own published pages, so treat this as a question to ask your local registry rather than something to assume either way.

Does the Public Trustee store wills, and is it free?

Often, and the terms differ by state. A few we were able to confirm from the bodies' own pages:

  • New South Wales. NSW Trustee and Guardian runs a service called WillSafe, which stores your will along with a power of attorney and an appointment of enduring guardian. It is free for clients who appoint NSW Trustee and Guardian as executor or attorney, with a fee applying otherwise. (Source: nsw.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)
  • Victoria. State Trustees describes its Victorian Will and Powers of Attorney Registry as a free service offered to all Victorians, without a requirement to appoint them. (Source: statetrustees.com.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)
  • Tasmania. The Public Trustee offers free storage of estate planning documents where those documents are prepared with the Public Trustee. (Source: publictrustee.tas.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)
  • Australian Capital Territory. The Public Trustee and Guardian will hold your current will in its Will Register for a one-off fee, quoted on its website at $228 per will, with further fees for searches, certified copies and withdrawal. (Source: ptg.act.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)

Queensland, South Australia, Western Australia and the Northern Territory all have public trustee bodies, and we could not read their current storage terms directly from their websites while writing this, so we are not going to quote figures we have not verified. A phone call to your own state's public trustee will settle it in a few minutes, and the question to ask is whether storage is free, and whether free depends on them preparing the will or being named as executor.

Is a bank safe deposit box a good idea?

It is secure, and security is not the only thing that matters here. The question worth asking is who can open the box, and when.

Banks set their own rules. BankSA, as one published example, says that a will held in a deceased customer's safe deposit box can be retrieved by an executor or an authorised nominee of the executor, in the presence of two bankers, and only once the bank has at least one of its accepted proofs of death.

Read that carefully and the awkward shape of it appears. The person who needs the box opened may be asked to show that they are the executor, and the document that shows they are the executor can be the one inside the box. It is not an unsolvable problem, and it is a delay at the exact moment your family has the least patience for one. If you do use a box, tell the bank in advance what its process is, and tell your executor what you were told.

What about a fireproof safe at home?

Moneysmart lists it as a legitimate option, alongside a solicitor, the courts and the Public Trustee. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026) Home storage keeps the will in your own hands, which suits people who would rather not lodge personal documents anywhere.

The catch is the one nobody thinks about until it happens. If the safe is locked and the combination died with you, the safe has done its job a little too well. Whatever you choose at home, the opening instructions need to sit somewhere your executor can reach.

Who actually needs to know where it is?

Your executor, first and foremost. NSW Trustee and Guardian's own recommendation is to tell your executor where your will is stored, so that the executor can contact them to retrieve it. (Source: nsw.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026) Moneysmart's wording is broader, saying to tell someone you trust where to find it. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 25 Sep 2026)

Telling somebody where the will is kept is not the same as telling them what is in it. You can hand over the location and say nothing about the contents, and most people find that a far easier conversation than the one they were dreading.

A short checklist you can work through this week

None of this is advice about your circumstances. It is the list of things that can be checked without an appointment or a fee.

  1. Put your hands on the original. Not the copy, not the scan. Confirm the signed document exists and that you know where it is right now.
  2. Check the storage is dry and secure. Damp cupboards, garages and car gloveboxes have all claimed wills.
  3. Ring your state's public trustee. Ask whether they store wills, what it costs, and whether free storage depends on them preparing the will or being named executor.
  4. Ask your solicitor whether they hold it. If a solicitor prepared the will, they may already have the original in safe custody, and your family will need the firm's name.
  5. Tell your executor the location. One sentence, said out loud, is enough.
  6. Write the location down somewhere your family will look. A will nobody can find is the problem this page exists to prevent.

Where does this leave your family?

A solicitor handles the legal corner well, and the will itself is genuinely worth paying for. The trouble is that the will is one document, and knowing where it lives is a separate piece of information that lives only in your head until you write it down. So is everything around it: which bank, which super fund, which insurer, who to ring first.
