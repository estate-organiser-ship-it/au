Binding Death Benefit Nomination: How It Works in Australia

Published 15 July 2026. Updated 2 October 2026 by Will Kimak.

A binding death benefit nomination is a written instruction to your super fund that, if valid, requires the fund to pay your super to the people you name when you die. Moneysmart, ASIC's consumer site, puts it simply: a binding nomination requires the super fund to follow your instructions, while a non-binding nomination guides the fund but still allows them to decide who receives your super. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026) It matters because the money in your super account does not automatically form part of your estate, so your will may not decide where it goes. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

This page covers who you can nominate, the signing and witness rules, why many binding nominations lapse after three years, and what happens if you have none. Fund rules differ, and the ATO notes that super funds can offer different options for death benefit nominations, so your own fund's form has the final word. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

Binding, non-binding and non-lapsing nominations

Moneysmart lists the main types. Not all super funds offer all of them. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

  • Binding nomination (lapsing): the super fund must pay your super benefit to your nominated eligible beneficiary. Moneysmart says these need to be renewed or changed every 3 years otherwise they expire. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)
  • Binding nomination (non-lapsing): the fund must pay your nominated eligible beneficiary, and Moneysmart says these do not expire. Only some funds offer them. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)
  • Non-binding nomination: this guides your super fund trustee, but the fund is not bound to follow these wishes. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)
  • Binding reversionary nomination: this can apply if you have turned your super into an income stream such as a pension. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

If you have a non-binding nomination or none at all, the ATO says the trustee of the fund may use their discretion to decide which dependant or dependants to pay the death benefit to. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

Who you can nominate

The list is set by law. The ATO says that if the fund rules allow a binding death benefit nomination, you can nominate one or more of your dependants and/or your legal personal representative, meaning the executor of your estate. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

"Dependant" has a specific meaning in super law. The ATO says you are a dependant of the deceased if, at the time of their death, you were their spouse or de facto spouse, a child of the deceased of any age, or a person in an interdependency relationship with them. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

If you want your super to reach someone outside that list, such as a sibling or a friend, the ATO suggests asking your fund about a binding nomination to your legal personal representative, so the payment goes to your estate and is dealt with under your will. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026) Moneysmart adds: if you plan to nominate your legal personal representative, make sure your will is up to date. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

The signing and witness rules

This is where binding nominations most often fail. The rules sit in regulation 6.17A of the Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations 1994, which says the notice must be signed and dated by the member in the presence of 2 witnesses, each of whom has turned 18, and neither of whom is a person mentioned in the notice. (Source: legislation.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

So the person you are leaving your super to cannot witness the form. Neither can anyone under 18. Your fund's own form will set out the exact steps, and Moneysmart says each fund can have different rules on when a binding nomination becomes invalid, set out in the form you complete. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

Why many binding nominations lapse after three years

The same regulation says that unless revoked sooner, a binding notice ceases to have effect at the end of 3 years after the day it was first signed, or last confirmed or amended, or at the end of a shorter period if the fund's rules fix one. (Source: legislation.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026) You can confirm, amend or revoke it by giving the trustee a written, signed and dated notice. (Source: legislation.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

APRA, the prudential regulator, expects funds that offer binding nominations to have a procedure to notify members at the appropriate time that their nomination is about to lapse. (Source: apra.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026) Do not rely on that letter reaching you, though. People move house and change email addresses, and a reminder in your own diary costs nothing.

Tax: who counts as a dependant is a different list

The ATO draws a line that surprises many families: superannuation law sets out who a death benefit is payable to, while taxation law sets out how a death benefit is taxed. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

The tax list is narrower for adult children. For tax purposes, the ATO says children over 18 years old must be financially dependent on the deceased to be considered a dependant. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026) How much tax applies to any particular payment is a question for the fund, the ATO or a licensed adviser.

Self-managed super funds

An SMSF works differently because the members are usually the trustees. The ATO says that without a binding nomination, the remaining trustees will decide how the benefits are distributed by considering the fund's trust deed and super laws. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026) If you have an SMSF, have the trust deed checked by a licensed adviser before relying on any nomination.

A two-minute check, and a place to record it

Log in to each super fund you have, including old ones from past jobs. Check whether a nomination is in place, whether it is binding or non-binding, whether it lapses, and the date it was signed. If you are not sure what your fund offers, the ATO's advice is to contact your super fund to find out what applies to your account. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

Then write it down: which funds, the member numbers, who you nominated and when it expires. Your executor will need exactly that list. Common questions about binding death benefit nominations

What is a binding death benefit nomination?

A written instruction to your super fund that, if valid, requires the fund to pay your super to the people you name. Moneysmart says a binding nomination requires the fund to follow your instructions, while a non-binding one only guides it. (Source: moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

Who can I nominate in a binding death benefit nomination?

One or more of your dependants and/or your legal personal representative, the executor of your estate, if your fund's rules allow binding nominations. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

How many witnesses does a binding death benefit nomination need?

Two. The regulations require the member to sign and date it in the presence of 2 witnesses who have each turned 18 and who are not named in the nomination. (Source: legislation.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

Does a binding death benefit nomination expire?

A lapsing one does. The regulations say it ceases after 3 years from when it was signed, confirmed or amended, or sooner if the fund's rules say so. Some funds offer non-lapsing binding nominations, which Moneysmart says do not expire. (Sources: legislation.gov.au and moneysmart.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

Does my will decide who gets my super?

Not by default. Moneysmart says the money in your super account does not automatically form part of your estate. A binding nomination to your legal personal representative is one way to have it paid to your estate and dealt with under your will. (Sources: moneysmart.gov.au and ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)

What happens if I have no nomination at all?

The ATO says that with a non-binding nomination or no nomination, the trustee of the fund may use their discretion to decide which dependant or dependants receive the death benefit. (Source: ato.gov.au, checked 2 Oct 2026)
